\begin{abstract}
Maintaining cleanliness in shared urban environments is a growing
operational challenge for municipalities, institutions, and civic
bodies. Conventional CCTV systems function primarily as passive
recording infrastructure and require continuous human supervision
to detect violations, making them resource-intensive and slow to
respond. This paper proposes Eco Life Buddy, an AI-powered CCTV
monitoring framework that autonomously identifies public cleanliness
violations and inappropriate public conduct using real-time video
analytics.

The system employs YOLO26 as its primary detection backbone, taking
advantage of its end-to-end NMS-free inference architecture,
dual-head design, and optimized edge-deployment characteristics.
Object detections are extended through temporal event analysis and
spatial proximity reasoning to interpret actions such as littering,
open dumping, and negligent waste disposal. A rule-based decision
module classifies observed events, archives annotated evidence
frames, and dispatches real-time alerts to enforcement personnel
through email and SMS channels.

Experimental evaluation on a custom dataset assembled from
real-world CCTV footage spanning diverse illumination conditions
and crowd densities demonstrates strong detection performance with
mean Average Precision at IoU 50 reaching 91.4 percent and
real-time throughput exceeding 30 frames per second on standard
GPU hardware. The proposed system reduces manual monitoring
workload, enhances detection reliability, and provides a scalable
foundation for public cleanliness enforcement in smart-city
deployments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rapid urbanization has introduced a sharp rise in the complexity
of managing cleanliness, hygiene, and civic conduct in public
areas. Violations such as littering, roadside dumping, and
improper waste placement are not only aesthetically damaging but
also carry direct consequences for public health, stormwater
management, and environmental sustainability \cite{b1}. The
United Nations Environment Programme estimates that over 2.1
billion tonnes of municipal solid waste are generated globally
each year, with a significant portion attributed to improper
disposal in outdoor public spaces \cite{b2}. Cities that lack
the ability to detect and respond to these violations in real
time often face a deteriorating cycle of non-compliance and
increasing remediation cost.

Most urban surveillance networks today consist of extensive
grids of IP cameras deployed across streets, parks, transport
terminals, and commercial zones. Despite this pervasive hardware
presence, the majority of deployments still rely on human
operators to review footage either after an incident is reported
or during scheduled monitoring shifts. This approach introduces
significant latency between the occurrence of a violation and
its acknowledgment, reducing deterrence effectiveness and
limiting accountability. Manual monitoring is also subject to
fatigue, distraction, and inconsistency, particularly when a
single operator is responsible for multiple concurrent camera
feeds \cite{b11}.

Recent advances in deep learning and computer vision have
created a practical path toward transforming passive CCTV
infrastructure into active intelligent monitoring systems.
Modern single-stage object detectors can identify visual targets
in complex scenes with accuracy and latency profiles suitable
for real-time video processing \cite{b4}. The introduction of
temporal sequence models has further enabled interpretation of
actions across multiple consecutive frames. These developments,
combined with the growing availability of edge-capable hardware,
make autonomous cleanliness surveillance a feasible and timely
research direction \cite{b12}.

From a smart-city perspective, the integration of intelligent
video analysis into existing surveillance infrastructure offers
additional value beyond simple violation detection. Continuous
cleanliness monitoring enables municipal authorities to build
spatial heat maps of high-frequency violation zones, allocate
cleaning crews proactively, and evaluate the impact of public
awareness campaigns over time. This data-driven governance
approach is increasingly recognized as a core component of
sustainable urban management \cite{b13}.

This paper introduces Eco Life Buddy, an end-to-end AI-based
CCTV monitoring system designed to automatically detect public
cleanliness violations and inappropriate conduct. Central to
the proposed design is the adoption of YOLO26 as the primary
detection engine \cite{b3}. Released in January 2026 by
Ultralytics, YOLO26 represents the latest evolution of the YOLO
model family and introduces several improvements for
deployment-centric applications: natively end-to-end inference
that eliminates post-processing Non-Maximum Suppression, removal
of the Distribution Focal Loss module for broader hardware
compatibility, the MuSGD optimizer for stable training
convergence, and up to 43 percent faster CPU throughput compared
to its predecessor YOLO11 \cite{b3}. These properties make
YOLO26 particularly well-suited for surveillance applications
requiring low-latency and hardware-agnostic deployment.

The major contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A real-time CCTV monitoring pipeline for detecting
      cleanliness and conduct violations using YOLO26-based
      object detection integrated with temporal event analysis
      and spatial reasoning.
\item A spatial proximity model that evaluates relationships
      between detected persons, waste objects, and designated
      disposal zones to reduce false positive violation reports.
\item A temporal consistency framework that tracks object and
      person trajectories across frames to confirm event-level
      violations before any alert is raised.
\item A lightweight rule-based decision engine that maps
      low-level detection outputs to semantic violation
      categories without requiring a separate classification
      network.
\item An evidence management and alert notification module
      that generates annotated snapshots, structured event
      logs, and multi-channel authority notifications.
\item A violation heatmap generation module that aggregates
      spatial event coordinates over configurable time periods
      to support data-driven municipal resource planning.
\item Experimental validation on a custom-collected CCTV
      dataset covering diverse real-world conditions with
      comprehensive ablation studies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Object Detection in Waste Monitoring}

The application of deep learning to visual recognition in
surveillance settings has evolved considerably over the past
decade. Early approaches relied on background subtraction and
handcrafted feature descriptors such as HOG and SIFT, but these
methods degraded significantly under environmental changes,
occlusion, and cluttered backgrounds \cite{b14}. The
introduction of deep convolutional networks provided a pathway
to more robust and generalizable visual feature extraction,
enabling models to learn hierarchical representations directly
from raw pixel data.

Single-stage detectors have become the dominant paradigm for
real-time video analytics. The YOLO architecture, first proposed
by Redmon et al., reformulates object detection as a single
regression problem over a uniform spatial grid, enabling
real-time inference through a single forward pass \cite{b4}.
Successive versions of the YOLO family have progressively
improved accuracy on standard benchmarks while maintaining
competitive inference speeds \cite{b5}. Liu et al.\ proposed
SSD, which achieves detection across multiple feature map
scales using predefined default boxes \cite{b6}.

In the domain of environmental monitoring, YOLO-family
detectors have been applied to waste classification, roadside
garbage detection, and municipal cleanliness assessment.
Nayfeh et al.\ demonstrated a two-stage YOLO-based pipeline
capable of classifying 16 distinct waste types from urban
street scenes \cite{b7}. Automated street waste detection
systems using YOLO-based models have reported mAP values
exceeding 96 percent on curated street datasets \cite{b8},
confirming the suitability of single-stage detectors for
public cleanliness tasks.

Beyond object-level classification, transformer-based
detection architectures such as DETR and its variants have
demonstrated competitive performance for small-object
detection scenarios, which are particularly relevant when
surveilling litter items in wide-angle CCTV views. However,
these models typically require substantially higher
computational resources at inference time compared to
YOLO-family detectors, limiting their suitability for
continuous multi-camera surveillance \cite{b15}.

\subsection{Temporal Modeling and Action Recognition}

Detecting a static waste object provides limited information
about whether a violation has actually occurred. A bottle
visible on the ground may result from littering by a nearby
person, accidental dropping, or pre-existing placement from
an earlier event. Accurate violation detection therefore
requires reasoning about the temporal evolution of the scene,
including human motion, object trajectory, and the sequence
of events leading to the observed state.

CNN-LSTM hybrid architectures have emerged as a widely adopted
strategy for video-based action recognition, where
convolutional layers extract per-frame spatial features and
LSTM units model temporal dependencies across sequential frames
\cite{b9}. This combination has achieved competitive accuracy
on standard benchmarks and has been extended to
surveillance-specific tasks including pedestrian behavior
analysis and anomaly detection. Variants employing bidirectional
LSTM units have further improved recognition accuracy on tasks
where future frame context aids disambiguation.

Alharbi et al.\ applied the MoViNet video classification model
to detect littering activities by both vehicles and pedestrians,
reporting strong accuracy on a custom-labeled dataset
\cite{b10}. Their work highlights the advantage of using
video-level classification rather than frame-level detection
alone, particularly for behaviors that unfold across multiple
frames. However, MoViNet-based systems require the full video
clip to be available before classification, which imposes a
buffering delay that is incompatible with the continuous
alerting requirements of real-time surveillance.

Optical flow methods such as FlowNet and PWC-Net have also been
employed as auxiliary inputs to action recognition models in
surveillance contexts, enabling explicit representation of
motion patterns between consecutive frames \cite{b16}.
While effective, the computational overhead of dense optical
flow estimation makes these approaches challenging to deploy
in multi-camera systems operating at full frame rate.

Graph convolutional networks applied to human skeleton pose
sequences have demonstrated strong performance for fine-grained
action classification, with the advantage of being less
sensitive to appearance variation \cite{b17}. The integration
of pose-based reasoning with object detection is an active
research direction that the proposed system identifies as a
promising avenue for future enhancement.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking in Surveillance}

Multi-object tracking is a fundamental component of any
surveillance system that needs to establish associations between
events occurring at different moments in time. Classical tracking
approaches include the Kalman filter combined with the Hungarian
algorithm for frame-to-frame assignment, as popularized by the
SORT algorithm \cite{b18}. Extensions such as DeepSORT augment
appearance-based re-identification features with motion
prediction to handle tracking through occlusion \cite{b19}.

More recent approaches employing transformer-based attention
mechanisms for joint detection and tracking have reported
state-of-the-art performance on public tracking benchmarks.
However, for the specific requirements of cleanliness
surveillance — namely tracking both persons and small waste
objects across modest time horizons in relatively constrained
scenes — lightweight centroid-based tracking strategies
augmented with IoU matching offer a favorable accuracy-to-cost
trade-off. The proposed system adopts such a lightweight
tracker to minimize the computational footprint of the tracking
layer, reserving processing headroom for multi-camera
parallelism.

\subsection{Integrated Smart Surveillance Systems}

Several prior systems have attempted to combine detection,
behavior understanding, and automated reporting into unified
smart surveillance frameworks. These systems generally fall into
two categories: research prototypes focused on a single task
such as waste detection, and broader platforms that handle
multiple violation types but lack real-time alerting and
evidence capture capabilities.

Park et al.\ described a CCTV-integrated anomaly detection
system for smart campuses that employed background subtraction
combined with a convolutional classifier to flag unauthorized
access events and debris accumulation \cite{b20}. While their
system demonstrated reliable detection under controlled lighting,
it did not incorporate temporal reasoning at the behavior level,
treating each frame independently. This limitation results in
high false-positive rates in dynamic outdoor environments where
stationary objects may appear and disappear due to pedestrian
occlusion.

A notable gap in the literature is the absence of end-to-end
systems that integrate an NMS-free detection backbone with
temporal event reasoning, zone-aware spatial analysis, and
operational alerting infrastructure in a single deployable
framework. The present work addresses this gap by proposing a
unified system built on YOLO26, whose end-to-end design and
edge-deployment efficiency make it well matched to the
requirements of continuous CCTV surveillance \cite{b3}.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Given a continuous video stream consisting of frames
$f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_T$ captured by a fixed surveillance
camera, the goal of the proposed system is to identify the
occurrence of a cleanliness or conduct violation at frame
time $t$ and to generate a corresponding alert with
supporting evidence.

Formally, a violation event $E$ is defined as a tuple:
\begin{equation}
E = \langle P,\; O,\; Z,\; \Delta t,\; \theta \rangle
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the detected person bounding box, $O$ is the
detected waste object bounding box, $Z$ is the designated
disposal zone, $\Delta t$ is the temporal window over which
the interaction is observed, and $\theta$ is a confidence
threshold that must be exceeded for the event to be classified
as a confirmed violation.

The spatial proximity condition between a person $P$ and a
waste object $O$ is formalized as:
\begin{equation}
d(P, O) = \sqrt{(x_P - x_O)^2 + (y_P - y_O)^2} \leq d_{pw}
\end{equation}
where $(x_P, y_P)$ and $(x_O, y_O)$ are the centroid
coordinates of the respective bounding boxes in pixel space,
and $d_{pw}$ is the configured proximity threshold.

The waste-bin proximity condition is similarly defined as:
\begin{equation}
d(O, B) = \sqrt{(x_O - x_B)^2 + (y_O - y_B)^2} > d_{wb}
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the nearest detected bin or disposal zone
boundary, and $d_{wb}$ is the acceptable proximity radius
within which placement is considered compliant.

A candidate violation is declared when both conditions
are satisfied simultaneously over a temporal window
$\Delta t$, and escalates to a confirmed event only when
the temporal consistency gate is satisfied.

\subsection{Violation Categories}

The system targets three primary violation categories:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Active Littering:} A person is observed directly
      placing or dropping a waste object outside a designated
      disposal area within a defined temporal window.
\item \textbf{Waste Abandonment:} An unattended waste object
      persists outside a disposal zone beyond a configurable
      duration threshold, suggesting intentional abandonment.
\item \textbf{Improper Disposal:} A waste object is deposited
      at an incorrect location even when a designated bin is
      present nearby, indicating disregard for available
      disposal infrastructure.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Assumptions}

The proposed system operates under the following explicit
scope assumptions: cameras are fixed-mount with known field
of view, camera positions and disposal zone boundaries are
configured per installation during system setup, all
participating cameras provide continuous video streams with
at least 15 frames per second, and frames are captured in
resolutions sufficient to resolve human-scale targets at
the nominal deployment distance. These assumptions are
representative of standard municipal CCTV deployments.

\subsection{Key Technical Challenges}

Automatic violation detection from CCTV video introduces
several interrelated challenges:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cluttered backgrounds:} Urban scenes contain
      stationary objects, shadows, and visually similar
      surface textures that may be confused with waste items,
      particularly in market and roadside environments.
\item \textbf{Small object size:} Litter items such as
      wrappers, cigarette packets, and straws occupy very few
      pixels in wide-angle CCTV views, stressing the recall
      of conventional detectors.
\item \textbf{Occlusion:} Crowds and overlapping objects
      can partially obscure both persons and waste items,
      interrupting track continuity and temporal reasoning.
\item \textbf{Variable illumination:} Day-to-night
      transitions and artificial lighting alter the visual
      signatures of detected objects, leading to performance
      degradation in low-light conditions without
      preprocessing compensation.
\item \textbf{Temporal ambiguity:} Distinguishing deliberate
      littering from accidental drops requires multi-frame
      analysis rather than single-frame reasoning, and the
      boundary between the two behaviors may be subtle.
\item \textbf{Camera angle variation:} Different mounting
      heights and tilt angles across camera installations
      require perspective-invariant proximity thresholds or
      per-camera calibration, adding configuration complexity
      in large-scale deployments.
\item \textbf{Multi-person scenes:} Correctly attributing a
      detected waste object to the responsible individual
      in scenes with multiple co-located pedestrians requires
      careful spatial-temporal assignment logic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

The proposed methodology follows a multi-stage event
understanding pipeline organized into six stages: frame
acquisition and preprocessing, YOLO26-based object detection,
multi-object tracking, spatial proximity analysis, temporal
consistency evaluation, and rule-based decision classification.
Each stage progressively refines the raw pixel-level video
signal into a structured, actionable violation event or a
non-event determination.

\subsection{Frame Acquisition and Preprocessing}

Video frames are captured at a configurable sampling rate
from live RTSP streams, file-based CCTV recordings, or
USB-connected cameras. The ingestion layer normalizes the
incoming frame rate using a token-bucket throttling mechanism,
ensuring that downstream processing operates at a stable
cadence regardless of stream jitter.

Each captured frame undergoes a four-step preprocessing
pipeline. First, the frame is resized to a target inference
resolution of $640 \times 640$ pixels using bilinear
interpolation. Second, for footage recorded under low or
artificial lighting, Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram
Equalization (CLAHE) is applied per luminance channel of
the YCbCr color representation to enhance local contrast
while suppressing noise amplification at homogeneous regions.
Third, a motion detection gate computes the per-pixel
absolute difference between the current frame and the
previous processed frame. If the aggregate motion score
falls below a configurable minimum threshold, the frame
is skipped to avoid wasteful inference on near-static
scenes. Fourth, selected frames are normalized using
the YOLO26 input standardization constants prior to model
inference.

The motion gating step reduces unnecessary inference
computation, particularly in overnight or low-traffic periods,
improving overall system efficiency without sacrificing
detection latency during active periods.

\subsection{YOLO26-Based Object Detection}

YOLO26 is adopted as the primary visual detection backbone.
Operating in its native end-to-end mode, it produces object
predictions directly without a separate NMS post-processing
stage, which reduces inference latency and simplifies the
downstream pipeline \cite{b3}. The removal of the NMS stage
is particularly beneficial in multi-camera deployments, as it
eliminates a traditionally non-parallelizable post-processing
bottleneck and enables consistent per-frame latency budgets.

The YOLO26-Small variant is selected as the default
configuration, offering a strong balance of detection accuracy
and inference efficiency suitable for continuous surveillance
workloads. The model employs a dual-head prediction design
that generates decoupled classification and regression outputs,
improving localization precision for small objects compared to
shared-head architectures. The Progressive Loss (ProgLoss)
and Spatial-Temporal Attention Loss (STAL) objectives
incorporated in YOLO26 training further improve small-object
recall, which is directly relevant to the detection of litter
items in wide-angle CCTV frames.

The model was fine-tuned using the MuSGD optimizer, which
combines the directional efficiency of SGD with the adaptive
curvature correction of the Muon optimizer, achieving faster
convergence and improved final accuracy compared to standard
SGD or Adam baselines on detection-focused fine-tuning tasks.

The detection stage is configured to identify four object
classes relevant to public cleanliness monitoring:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Person} -- All visible individuals in the
      camera field of view, regardless of posture or clothing.
\item \textit{Waste Item} -- Bottles, plastic bags, food
      packaging, paper, cigarette butts, and other discarded
      objects. Annotations use a unified waste category to
      simplify detection while retaining sufficient semantic
      granularity for violation reasoning.
\item \textit{Bin} -- Designated waste receptacles of any
      type, including public bins, recycling containers,
      and covered waste enclosures.
\item \textit{Disposal Zone} -- Permitted disposal areas
      defined per-camera as annotated region-of-interest
      polygons during installation calibration.
\end{itemize}

Detections below a minimum confidence threshold of 0.45 are
discarded prior to tracking to suppress low-quality predictions
in cluttered scene regions.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking}

Detected bounding boxes across consecutive frames are
associated into persistent object tracks using a lightweight
centroid-based tracker augmented with Intersection over Union
(IoU) matching. The tracker operates independently for person
and waste-item class streams, maintaining per-class identity
tables to prevent cross-class assignment errors.

At each frame, detected bounding boxes for a given class are
matched to existing tracks by solving a bipartite assignment
problem using the Hungarian algorithm \cite{b18}. The cost
matrix is constructed as a weighted combination of centroid
Euclidean distance and one-minus-IoU, with the distance
component normalized by the diagonal length of the frame.
Tracks that fail to receive a matching detection in a given
frame are placed in a tentative absence state rather than
immediately terminated. Track continuity is maintained through
brief occlusion gaps using a maximum absence tolerance of
15 frames, after which unmatched tracks are retired. This
tolerance enables downstream temporal reasoning modules to
maintain continuous event evaluation across momentary tracking
interruptions caused by occlusion or brief inference misses.

Each active track maintains a sliding trajectory buffer
storing the centroid position history over the most recent
30 frames, which feeds into the spatial proximity analysis
and temporal consistency modules.

\subsection{Spatial Proximity Analysis}

For each detected waste object track, the spatial proximity
model evaluates two binary conditions within each frame of
the analysis window.

The person-waste proximity condition assesses whether any
tracked person has been within pixel-distance threshold
$d_{pw}$ of the waste object centroid at any point within
the preceding temporal window $\Delta t$. This condition
is evaluated over the trajectory buffer of all active
person tracks rather than only the current frame positions,
allowing the system to establish association between a
person and a waste item even when the person has moved
away from the deposition point by the time the waste item
is fully confirmed.

The waste-bin condition assesses whether the waste object's
centroid falls within a designated disposal zone polygon or
within distance $d_{wb}$ of a detected bin centroid. Zone
boundaries are defined as camera-specific polygon annotations
stored in the configuration file and loaded at system startup.
If a waste object lies within a designated zone, it is
considered compliantly placed and suppressed from violation
analysis regardless of any associated person-proximity
condition.

When a waste object satisfies the person-proximity condition
and fails the waste-bin condition, it is flagged as a
candidate violation and its associated person track is
recorded as the suspected responsible entity. The candidate
is then passed to the temporal consistency module for
duration-based confirmation.

\subsection{Temporal Consistency and Event Reasoning}

To prevent transient detections or momentary scene states
from generating spurious alerts, a temporal consistency gate
requires that a candidate violation persist for a minimum
observation window $\tau_{\min}$ before escalation to the
decision engine. The gate maintains a per-candidate state
machine with three states:

\begin{equation}
\text{State} =
\begin{cases}
\text{OBSERVE}   & \text{if no proximity condition is met} \\
\text{CANDIDATE} & \text{if proximity condition is satisfied} \\
\text{CONFIRMED} & \text{if duration} \geq \tau_{\min}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

A running frame counter increments while the candidate
conditions remain satisfied, and resets to zero if conditions
cease to hold before the minimum window is reached. This
prevents accidental or coincidental proximity — such as a
person briefly pausing near a pre-existing waste item — from
being classified as an active littering event.

Additionally, the module enforces a cooldown period of
$\tau_{cd}$ frames per track pair after a confirmed violation
is generated. During the cooldown, the same person-waste track
combination is suppressed from generating a second alert,
preventing repeated notifications for a single sustained
violation event.

\subsection{Rule-Based Decision Engine}

The decision engine receives confirmed event states from the
temporal consistency module along with contextual metadata and
maps each event to one of four outcome labels:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Active Littering:} Person track recently in
      proximity with confirmed waste item outside a disposal
      zone, with the waste item appearing within the current
      temporal window (i.e., the object was not present
      prior to the person's arrival).
\item \textbf{Waste Abandonment:} Waste item persists
      outside a disposal zone beyond the abandonment
      threshold $\tau_{ab}$ without any associated active
      person track in proximity.
\item \textbf{Improper Disposal:} Waste item deposited
      in non-compliant proximity to a visible bin, satisfying
      both person-proximity and bin-proximity conditions
      simultaneously but failing the zone-compliance condition.
\item \textbf{Inconclusive:} Conditions partially met but
      insufficient evidence to assign a primary violation
      category. Logged for optional manual review without
      generating an automated alert.
\end{itemize}

The decision logic is implemented as an interpretable
rule set rather than a trainable classifier, which allows
operators to adjust thresholds and zone boundaries through
configuration without retraining the detection model. Each
confirmed classification is enriched with metadata including
timestamp, camera identifier, violation category, detection
confidence, associated person track ID, and geographic
coordinates derived from camera calibration parameters.

\subsection{Violation Heatmap Aggregation}

Beyond per-event alerting, the system maintains a
spatial accumulator that records the pixel coordinates of
confirmed violations for each camera over configurable
aggregation periods (hourly, daily, weekly). A Gaussian
kernel density estimate is computed over accumulated
violation coordinates to produce a smoothed spatial heatmap.
These heatmaps are exported as overlay images aligned to
camera fields of view and can be composited onto
georeferenced city maps when camera position metadata is
available. Municipal enforcement teams can use these
visualizations to identify persistent violation hotspots,
optimize bin placement, and prioritize patrol schedules.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The Eco Life Buddy system is organized into seven hierarchical
functional layers, as illustrated conceptually below. Each
layer communicates with adjacent layers through well-defined
interfaces, enabling modular replacement of individual
components without disrupting the overall pipeline.

\subsection{Video Acquisition Layer}

This layer interfaces with CCTV cameras via RTSP streams,
local video files, or USB-connected cameras. It handles
connection management, stream buffering, frame-rate
normalization, and automatic stream reconnection on network
interruption. Multiple camera feeds are managed concurrently
through a thread-pool architecture, with each stream assigned
an independent processing pipeline running on a dedicated
thread. Thread-safe queues buffer decoded frames between
the acquisition thread and the downstream preprocessing
thread, absorbing short-term throughput variations while
maintaining low end-to-end latency.

\subsection{Preprocessing Layer}

Raw frames are passed through the preprocessing pipeline for
resolution standardization, optional CLAHE contrast
enhancement, and motion-based activity gating. The motion
gate computes a per-pixel absolute difference between
consecutive frames and aggregates the result into a scalar
activity score. Frames below the configurable activity
threshold are bypassed, and the frame counter for the
temporal consistency module is advanced accordingly.

\subsection{Detection and Tracking Layer}

The YOLO26-based detector processes normalized frames and
produces bounding box predictions, class labels, and
confidence scores in a single forward pass without a
separate NMS stage. Batch inference is supported for
installations with multiple co-located cameras, where
frames from several streams can be grouped into a single
GPU batch to improve hardware utilization. The multi-object
tracker receives per-frame detections and maintains
identity-persistent tracks for all active entities.
Track state tables are maintained in shared memory accessible
to both the detection-tracking layer and the downstream
reasoning layer, eliminating serialization overhead.

\subsection{Spatial and Temporal Reasoning Layer}

This layer implements the proximity analysis and temporal
consistency modules described in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
It maintains a rolling event buffer per camera stream and
evaluates candidate violation states across frames,
handling transient gaps due to partial occlusion or
momentary inference misses. State transitions are logged
to an internal event journal that supports post-hoc
debugging and system auditing.

\subsection{Decision and Classification Layer}

The rule-based decision engine receives confirmed event
states and produces labeled violation outcomes. Metadata
including timestamp, camera identifier, violation category,
confidence score, and associated track identifiers are
appended to a structured SQLite event database for audit
and reporting purposes. The decision layer also feeds
confirmed violation coordinates to the heatmap aggregation
module for spatial analysis.

\subsection{Alert and Evidence Management Layer}

Upon confirmed violation classification, this layer retrieves
the relevant frame from the evidence frame buffer, annotates
it with bounding box overlays, violation labels, confidence
scores, and a timestamp watermark, then archives the annotated
image to persistent storage organized by camera, date, and
violation category. Notifications are dispatched through
email using the SMTP protocol and SMS via an integrated
messaging API. Each notification includes the violation
category, timestamp, camera location identifier, and a
reference identifier linking to the archived evidence frame
accessible through the management dashboard.

An alert deduplication policy prevents repeated notifications
for the same event. If a confirmed violation remains active
across multiple consecutive confirmation cycles, no additional
alerts are issued until the violation state clears and
re-triggers above the confirmation threshold.

\subsection{Management Dashboard Layer}

A lightweight web-based dashboard provides enforcement
personnel and system administrators with access to the real-time
camera feeds, event log, evidence archive, system health
metrics, and violation heatmaps. The dashboard is implemented
as a Flask-based web application with a responsive frontend
and supports role-based access control, distinguishing between
read-only monitoring roles and configuration roles with access
to zone boundary editing and threshold adjustment. System
health telemetry including per-camera frame processing rate,
detection latency, and alert dispatch success rate is
displayed in a live status panel, enabling rapid identification
of camera outages or processing bottlenecks.

\section{Dataset and Implementation}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Dataset Collection}

A custom dataset was assembled from CCTV footage collected
across five public locations in Chennai, India: a municipal
bus stand, a public park, a market street, a college campus
entrance, and a roadside pedestrian zone. Footage was recorded
using existing fixed-mount surveillance cameras over a
three-week collection period covering both daytime and
night-time conditions, with crowd densities ranging from
sparse pedestrian traffic to moderately crowded scenes.
Recording sessions were distributed across different times
of day to capture variation in lighting conditions, pedestrian
volume, and seasonal waste patterns.

\subsubsection{Annotation Protocol}

Object-level annotations were performed using a dedicated
labeling tool with a team of four trained annotators following
a standardized labeling guide. Annotation quality was
maintained through a two-pass review process in which a second
annotator independently verified all bounding boxes, and
disagreements were resolved by majority vote with a third
reviewer. Inter-annotator agreement, measured using the
standard COCO evaluation protocol at IoU threshold 0.50,
reached 94.3 percent across all object classes, confirming
the consistency of the annotation process.

Behavior-level ground truth was annotated at the clip level
by two independent raters who assigned one of four event
labels: proper disposal, active littering, waste abandonment,
and background activity. Clips with conflicting labels between
raters were reviewed by a third annotator to determine the
final label. A total of 1,840 annotated video clips ranging
from 3 to 10 seconds in duration were produced.

\subsubsection{Class Distribution}

A total of 14,200 annotated frames were prepared across four
object categories: person, waste item, bin, and disposal zone.
The dataset exhibits a natural imbalance reflecting real-world
frequency: person instances are the most frequent class,
followed by waste items at roughly 60 percent of person
frequency, with bin and disposal zone instances appearing at
lower rates commensurate with their density in the recorded
environments. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the dataset

\subsubsection{Data Augmentation}

To improve model generalization across the range of capture
conditions present in deployment environments, a comprehensive
augmentation strategy was applied during training. Augmentations
included random horizontal flipping with probability 0.5,
HSV color-space jitter applied to hue, saturation, and value
channels independently, mosaic composition that combines four
training images into a single synthetic training sample,
random perspective and affine transformations to simulate
camera angle variation, and random-scale crop operations. All
augmentations were applied online within the YOLO26 training
pipeline using the Ultralytics data augmentation module.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

The system was implemented using Python 3.10. The detection
model was trained and deployed using the Ultralytics library
version 26.0.0 for YOLO26 model training and inference. The
YOLO26-Small variant was fine-tuned for 100 epochs on the
custom dataset using the MuSGD optimizer with an initial
learning rate of 0.01 and cosine annealing learning rate
scheduling with a final learning rate ratio of 0.01. An
early stopping criterion with a patience of 15 epochs was
applied to prevent overfitting on the training set.

Training was conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090
GPU with 24 GB VRAM, batch size 16, and input resolution of
$640 \times 640$ pixels. Inference was performed using the
TensorRT 10 engine compiled with FP16 precision for
GPU-accelerated deployments, and ONNX Runtime for CPU-only
deployments. The multi-object tracker was implemented in
NumPy with the SciPy Hungarian algorithm solver. The alert
dispatcher uses the Python \texttt{smtplib} library for
email notifications and the Twilio REST API for SMS delivery.
The management dashboard is implemented with Flask 3.0 and
a Vanilla JavaScript frontend with Chart.js for live
telemetry visualization.

The waste item class yielded a slightly lower mAP compared to

waste bins relative to surrounding urban scene elements.
The disposal zone class, annotated as region-of-interest
polygons, showed slightly lower recall attributable to the
diversity of zone shapes and sizes across the five recording
locations.

deposition action. The waste abandonment category showed
slightly lower recall, as some short abandonment events that

\subsection{System Scalability}

Multi-camera scalability was evaluated on a test server
equipped with two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. Using batch inference
with a group size of 4 frames (one from each of four
simultaneous camera streams), the system sustained 29.8 FPS
per camera across 4 concurrent streams on a single GPU,
and 28.7 FPS per camera across 8 concurrent streams using
both GPUs. End-to-end alert latency (from frame capture to
notification dispatch) averaged 1.8 seconds under the 8-camera
configuration, which is well within the operational requirement
for effective enforcement response. These results confirm that
a single commodity GPU server can serve the monitoring
requirements of moderate-scale deployments (8 or more camera
zones) without specialized infrastructure.

\subsection{Robustness Under Challenging Conditions}

Additional evaluations were conducted on isolated subsets of
the test data to characterize performance under specific
challenging conditions.

\textbf{Night-time and artificial lighting:} Night-time footage
recorded under sodium vapor and LED street lighting resulted
in a 3.2 percent reduction in waste item recall compared to
daytime scenes. The CLAHE preprocessing step partially
mitigated this degradation; disabling CLAHE on night-time
footage alone increased the waste item recall drop to 6.7
percent.

\textbf{High-density crowds:} Under high pedestrian density
conditions, person-to-waste proximity analysis became less
discriminative due to tracker occlusion across overlapping
individuals, contributing to an elevated false alarm rate of
7.1 percent for active littering relative to the 4.1 percent
observed under normal density. Track loss and re-identification
errors were identified as the primary contributing factor.

\textbf{Adverse weather appearance effects:} A subset of
footage captured under overcast conditions with diffuse
lighting and wet reflective surfaces showed a modest 1.8
percent reduction in waste item precision, attributed to
specular surface reflections on wet ground creating false
detections. This suggests that weather-specific preprocessing
may offer additional robustness gains in climates with
frequent rain.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}

\subsection{Practical Deployment Considerations}

The Eco Life Buddy system was designed with operational
deployment in mind from the outset. The configuration-driven
approach to zone boundary definition and threshold
parameterization enables installation at new camera sites
without model retraining, reducing the expertise barrier
for municipal IT teams. The modular layer architecture
allows individual components — such as the detector, tracker,
or alert dispatcher — to be upgraded independently as
improved models or communication integrations become available.

One practical consideration for large-scale deployments is
the management of the evidence archive. At a sustained
detection rate in busy public areas, annotated evidence frames
can accumulate at significant volume. The current
implementation applies a configurable retention policy that
archives frames for confirmed violations for 90 days and
deletes inconclusive event frames after 7 days, balancing
evidentiary needs with storage capacity requirements.

\subsection{Privacy and Ethical Considerations}

Public surveillance systems that capture and analyze footage
of individuals must be designed and operated in compliance
with applicable data protection regulations. The Eco Life
Buddy system addresses this requirement through several
design choices: the system does not perform facial recognition
or individual identification, treating detected persons
solely as anonymous position tracks. Evidence frames stored
for confirmed violations contain the full annotated image
but are encrypted at rest and accessible only to authorized
enforcement personnel through the role-based dashboard.
Camera placement and the operational scope of the system
should be disclosed to the public through appropriate
signage as required by applicable local regulations.

Future work will investigate privacy-preserving preprocessing
techniques such as person silhouette substitution prior to
evidence archiving, which would further reduce the personally
identifiable content in stored footage while retaining
sufficient information for evidentiary review.

\subsection{Limitations}

Despite the strong experimental performance, several
limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the custom dataset,
while covering five representative locations, was collected
exclusively in Chennai, India. Model generalization to
significantly different urban environments, waste types,
or camera hardware configurations has not been validated.
Second, the person-to-violation attribution logic assumes
that the nearest person within the proximity window is the
responsible individual, which can produce incorrect
attributions in dense crowd scenarios. Third, the system
currently operates as a single-camera analyzer without
cross-camera event fusion, meaning that a violation event
that begins in one camera's field of view and concludes in
another's cannot be correlated as a single event. These
limitations define concrete directions for future research.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

This paper presented Eco Life Buddy, an AI-based CCTV
monitoring system for the automatic detection and reporting
of public cleanliness and conduct violations. The framework
integrates YOLO26-based object detection, multi-object
tracking, spatial proximity analysis, temporal consistency
evaluation, and a rule-based decision engine into a unified
real-time pipeline. An evidence management and alert
notification module completes the operational loop by
archiving annotated violation frames and dispatching
multi-channel notifications to enforcement personnel. A
violation heatmap aggregation module provides data-driven
spatial intelligence to support proactive municipal resource
planning.

The adoption of YOLO26 as the primary detection backbone
contributes directly to the system's practical deployability
through its end-to-end NMS-free inference, DFL-free
architecture for broad hardware compatibility, improved
small-object detection through ProgLoss and STAL loss
objectives, and MuSGD optimizer-driven training efficiency.
Experimental results on a custom real-world CCTV dataset
demonstrate reliable detection at mAP$_{50}$ of 93.0 percent,
event-level violation F1 of 0.892, and real-time GPU
throughput exceeding 30 frames per second. Ablation studies
confirm the independent contribution of the temporal
consistency gate and spatial proximity modules to overall
system reliability.

Future work will explore integration of pose-estimation cues
from the YOLO26-Pose variant to further enhance behavior
interpretation and enable finer-grained action discrimination.
Additional directions include expansion of violation categories
to cover noise nuisance and pedestrian encroachment,
privacy-preserving evidence processing to meet evolving data
protection requirements in public deployments, multi-camera
event correlation strategies for tracking violations that
span multiple camera fields of view, and investigation of
lightweight on-device deployment on NVIDIA Jetson or
similar embedded edge hardware to reduce infrastructure
cost for smaller municipal budgets.
